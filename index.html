My Money Was Stolen at Home. So I Built an App.

How a painful experience at home inspired a safer way to manage money.

When I went home to South Sudan, my money was stolen.

It was cash, of course. It was in my bag, then it wasn't. There was no transaction to trace, no account to freeze, no one to call. It was simply gone.

At first, I felt it as a personal loss. But the more I thought about it, the more I realized how ordinary my story was. In South Sudan, many of us live with cash. We carry it, hide it, count it twice, and hope. Saving, sending, and spending money all depend on holding paper in our hands, and that means one bad moment can erase months of work.

I did not want that to be normal anymore.

Starting before I was ready

I began building a money app before I had a salary. There was no budget and no safety net, only a problem I could not stop thinking about. Waiting until I was comfortable would have meant waiting while the same thing kept happening to people around me.

Being without a steady income also kept me honest. I could not afford to build things nobody needed. Every feature had to earn its place.

It starts with listening

Building a money app starts with more than writing code. It starts with listening to people, understanding the problem they face, and making sure the technology fits into their everyday lives.

That idea shaped how I approached the whole project. I wanted the experience to be clear. I wanted the important actions to be easy to find, so that someone opening the app for the first time would not feel lost or intimidated. Money is already stressful. The tool you use to manage it should not add to that.

Most of all, I wanted to build around real needs, not around what looked impressive. The theft taught me what one of those needs is: a safer place for money than a pocket or a bag.

A product is never finished

The other lesson was that the first version is only the beginning. People will use what you build in ways you did not expect, and they will get confused by things that seemed obvious to you. That is not failure. That is feedback.

So you learn from people, fix what is unclear, and keep building.

Why it matters

I built this app because of one painful experience, but I kept building it because of what that experience revealed. Many South Sudanese rely on cash, and with cash comes risk. If technology can reduce that risk in a way that fits naturally into daily life, then it is worth the effort, even before the salary arrives.

I am still learning, and the app is still growing. But I know why I started.

Building a money app starts with more than writing code. It starts with listening to people, understanding the problem they face, and making sure the technology fits into their everyday lives.

That idea shaped the way I approached this project: keep the experience clear, make the important actions easy to find, and build around real needs. It also taught me that a useful product is never finished after the first version. You learn from people, improve what is confusing, and keep building.
